### SIM 1 — Brown as Non-Spectral Color: Perceptual Science

**Claim:** Brown has no dedicated wavelength in the electromagnetic spectrum and exists only as a context-dependent perception produced by a living nervous system, making it structurally unique among all colors in the GAIA canon.

**Verification:**

Color vision science and colorimetry:
- The visible spectrum (~380–700 nm) contains no “brown” wavelength. Every named spectral color (red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet) corresponds to a range of electromagnetic frequencies. Brown does not.
- Brown is produced when the visual cortex receives low-luminance orange (~590–620 nm) in the context of higher-luminance surroundings. The contrast between the dark-orange signal and its brighter context is what the brain interprets as “brown.”
- Isolate the same low-luminance orange pixel on a black background: it appears orange. Put it on a bright background: it appears brown. **The color is entirely context-generated.**
- This is demonstrable using the Adelson checker-shadow illusion family and color constancy experiments. The same physical wavelength produces different color percepts depending on surrounding luminance context.
- No instrument that measures only wavelength can detect “brown” as a distinct signal. It requires a nervous system with brightness-comparison circuitry (retinal ganglion cells with center-surround receptive fields) embedded in a real-world context.

**Result: Brown’s non-spectral, context-dependent nature is verified by perceptual science. The Terra Tablet’s first law is grounded in neuroscience and colorimetry.**
